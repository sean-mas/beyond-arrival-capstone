Parents carry more guilt about this than about anything else in the move, so the honest
version is worth having.

The research base is thinner than the volume of writing about it suggests. One
systematic review screened over nine thousand records and found only fourteen studies
that met its criteria. Much of what circulates about children who grow up between
cultures is trade writing rather than evidence, and it tends to be either far more
reassuring or far more alarming than anything that has been measured.

What has been measured points at three things, and two are within your reach. Peer
acceptance matters most. School quality matters. And parental support matters.
Separately, a study of 324 children found that age, how the child was doing at school,
and the child's own attitude toward the move predicted how they adjusted.

## What people actually do

Treat the school situation as the main lever, because in the research it is the one that
carries most weight and the one you can actually change. That includes asking the
district school office what language support exists before accepting a placement.

Get the child into something local and repeated outside school, for the same reason it
works for adults. A Sportverein, a music school, the same weekly thing. Peer contact
that recurs is what turns classmates into friends.

Take the child's own view of the move seriously as information rather than managing it
as a mood. It predicts adjustment, which means it is worth asking about directly and
more than once.

## What to expect, and when to ask for a person

Most children do adapt. It commonly takes a year, it is usually harder before it is
easier, and friendships lag the language by months. If something has changed in your
child and stayed changed for weeks, that is a conversation for a person rather than for
us.
