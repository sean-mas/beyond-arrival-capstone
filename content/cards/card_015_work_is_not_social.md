If you came from a place where work also supplied your friends, this is the adjustment
nobody briefs you on, and it hits the person with the job rather than the person without
one.

Here the separation is deliberate. Colleagues are colleagues, friends are friends.
People are perfectly friendly, they will help you, and then at six o'clock they go home
to a life you are not part of, and none of that is a judgement of you. Someone with long
experience of both systems put it as Germans do make friends at work, but far more
slowly than in the UK.

The practical consequence matters more than the cultural observation. If you are relying
on the office for a social life you have outsourced it to a system that does not provide
one, and you notice around month four when the novelty of the job has worn off and there
is still nobody to call on a Saturday.

## What people actually do

Build the social side outside working hours and on a schedule, exactly as someone with
no job here would have to. A Verein, a course, a regular fixture. The person with the
job usually has less time and therefore needs the fixed slot more, not less.

Take the invitations that do exist literally and early. Lunch, the Betriebsausflug, the
Weihnachtsfeier, a colleague's Verein. These are the openings, and they are narrower and
more scheduled than the after-work drift you may be used to.

Ask a colleague what they do outside work rather than suggesting a drink. The Verein
route in is usually through someone who is already a member.

## What to expect

Work adjustment and social adjustment are separate dimensions in the research and move
independently. Doing well at work tells you nothing about the other one.
