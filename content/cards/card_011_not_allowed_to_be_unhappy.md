From the outside this move looks like good fortune. A salary, a new country, a life
other people would take. So when it is hard there is nowhere to put that. People
describe being asked why they set a career aside, and describe feeling like an
accessory to somebody else's job. The recurring image is confinement: a
comfortable life you are not free to leave. The
pattern on these boards is that people apologise before they complain, and then
complain anyway.

Two things are true at the same time. The move may well have been right for your
family. And you may have given up more than anyone acknowledged, for something that has
not arrived yet. Research on accompanying partners describes exactly this as a threat to
identity, distinct from the logistics, and finds that partners regularly report being
under-supported by the organisation that sent the family. So the sense that this was not
properly accounted for is not something you invented.

## What people actually do

Say it to your partner, in those words, once. Not as an accusation and not as a
request to go home. The most common report on these boards is that it had never been
said out loud, and that saying it went better than expected. The thing being managed
alone is usually the thing making it heavier.

Separate the two questions when you think about it. Whether the move was right for the
family is one question. Whether your part of it is working is a different one, and it
has its own answers, most of which are about structure and work rather than about
attitude.

Find one person who is in the same position. Not to complain with, though that helps.
Because the double bind is much easier to carry when someone else recognises it without
needing it explained.

## What does not work

Arguing yourself out of it. The feeling is a response to a real loss, and treating it
as ingratitude to be corrected is what keeps people silent for months.
