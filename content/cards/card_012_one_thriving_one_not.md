The same move can be the best year of one person's life and the worst of the other's,
and this is the thing almost nobody plans for.

The reason is structural rather than about character. One of you walks into a ready made
day: colleagues, a purpose, a reason to use the language, progress you can see. The
other walks into an empty flat and a list of offices. One of you is told constantly that
the move was a success. The plainest version of it on these
boards is one partner thriving while the other feels nothing at all.

Two findings matter here. The partners' adjustments are genuinely linked, including
whether the family stays, so this is not one person's private problem to solve alone.
And in a study of expatriates and spouses, wanting to leave early was not predicted by
how well someone had adjusted, but by how satisfied they were with the arrangement
overall.

## What people actually do

Change the question. "Why have you not settled yet" has no answer and puts it on one
person. "What would have to be different for this to be worth it for you too" has
answers, and they are usually concrete: a course, childcare, a return to work, a fixed
evening that is yours, a trip home already booked.

Put it in the diary rather than waiting for a good moment. Couples who handle this well
tend to have a regular, deliberately unremarkable conversation about it, rather than one
large conversation once things are bad.

Make the employed partner's time an actual variable. Whether they get home at six or at
eight changes the other person's week more than any advice does, and it is usually
negotiable in a way people do not test.

## What does not work

Waiting for the gap to close by itself. It is produced by the structure of your days,
so it closes when the structure changes and not before.
